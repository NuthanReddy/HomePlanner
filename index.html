Which one is mine?
Pick B (the common case) if your plot came out of an approved layout — an HMDA, DTCP or GHMC-sanctioned layout with an LP number on the layout plan, regular rectangular plots, and planned roads. Almost every plot bought in a modern venture, gated community or registered layout is Category B.
  • Your sale deed or link documents refer to a plot number in an approved layout
  • The layout has an LP / LRS number
  • Roads in the colony are laid out in a regular grid
Pick A only if your plot lies inside an area the local body has formally notified in Annexure-I of GO 168 as an old / existing built-up area, congested area, settlement, or gram kantam / abadi — the historic village or basti core that predates the municipal layout.
  • Irregular plot shapes and narrow winding lanes
  • Title traced through revenue / gram kantam records rather than a layout plot number
  • No approved layout for the area at all
Why it matters: it only changes the outcome when your road is under 9 m. There, Category A caps you at 10 m height while Category B allows 12 m for an individual residential building. On a 9 m road or wider the two behave the same.

How to confirm: the notified Annexure-I list is maintained by the local body. Ask the GHMC circle Town Planning office for your ward, or check the layout status on TG-bPASS. If your plot is in an approved layout, you are Category B — don't guess A.

Scaling Analysis

Legend = aspect ratio (frontage : depth). Each curve holds the shape constant and grows the plot, at the road width and settings selected above. Steps are the Table III band boundaries.
1 · Percentage of plot lost to setbacks
2 · Usable built-up area — footprint × floors permitted, stilt excluded
3 · Built-up index — usable built-up ÷ plot area (effective FAR achieved)
4 · All-in cost per sq ft of usable built-up — the value-for-money index. Lower is better.
Chart 3 is the one to judge value by: it is the built-up area you actually get per unit of land, so it nets off both the setback loss and the number of floors the plot unlocks. A plot band that costs you more setback can still win if it buys an extra floor — and the reverse. Squarer plots (1:1) always beat narrow deep plots at the same area, because the side setback is charged against both long edges. The highlighted aspect ratios are the derived optima for Table III bands at an abutting road of 12 m or less, where the front setback is at its floor; on wider roads the front setback rises to 4, 5, 6 or 7.5 m and pushes the optimum deeper. Rule of thumb: when the front setback exceeds the side setback the optimum is deeper than wide; when the side setback exceeds the front it is wider than deep; they are equal at square. Shape gains are small — typically 0.1–0.8% more built-up versus a square plot, rising to about 3% only for the smallest plots. Plot-size band and road width matter far more than shape.

Optimal Plot Shape

For a rectangular plot of fixed area, the optimum depth-to-frontage ratio is k* = (f + s) / 2s, where f is the front setback and s is the Table III side/rear setback. The front setback is charged once, while the side setback is charged twice, so the contest is f + s versus 2s. On roads up to 12 m, the optimum flips from deeper to wider at around 500 sq m as side/rear setbacks overtake the front setback. The gains are small — usually only about 0.1–0.8% versus a square plot — so shape matters far less than the plot-size band and road width. Crossing a band boundary can cost more than any shape optimisation gains.

How % Lost Varies With Plot Size

Each plot assumed at a 1 : 1.6 frontage : depth ratio, at the tallest height permitted for that band on the selected road, with no tot-lot deduction.

Watch the band boundaries. Setbacks step up at 200, 300, 500, 750 and 1000 sq m. Buying slightly more land can push you into the next band and leave less buildable footprint — 200 sq m keeps a 1.0 m side setback, 201 sq m jumps to 1.5 m plus a deeper front. Check the rows either side of your size before committing.
